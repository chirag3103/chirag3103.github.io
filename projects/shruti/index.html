The instrument

Eight strings. Three that sing.

A Mohan veena is a slide guitar adapted for Indian classical music: three main strings that carry the melody, three rhythm strings (chikari) and two drones. Shruti maps each raga across the full neck of the three main strings; the others stay hidden until you switch them on.

Each raga comes with its ascending and descending lines, its two most important notes, and its mood and time of day. Touch any note and it plays at that exact pitch.

Once you know it, the board stops being a diagram and becomes a map of the raga: home on every string, the notes to lean on, and where to go next.

Key decision: the fewest marks that say the most

A raga isn't just a set of notes. Sa is home, and two notes carry it: the vadi, the king, and the samvadi, its queen. Practice is about finding them fast, on any string, mid-phrase.

On the harmonium that was hard but fundamentally easy: the keys stand out by themselves, so lighting the right ones is enough. On the frets there are no keys, only strings and wires, and it took five rounds to get right. Every round added colour, and every round got harder to read with the instrument on my lap. The version I kept says it with almost nothing.

Where it began

The harmonium

Shruti started as a way to play ragas on the harmonium. The keys were already in front of me; what I wanted was to see a raga on them: which keys belong, which ones matter most, and how its lines walk up and down.

So the first Shruti did exactly that. Pick a raga, watch its notes light up on the keys, and hear its lines walked note by note. It still lives inside the app, one tap away from the frets.
